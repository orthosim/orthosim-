01 / THE PROBLEM

We often build first.
Then we find out.

In orthotics and prosthetics, a clinical decision can lead directly to fabrication. Only after fitting can we observe how the intervention changes the patient's movement.

If the outcome is not what we expected, the device may need modification, reinforcement, replacement or an entirely different approach. That costs time, materials, money — and another patient visit.

“What if we could test the intervention virtually before we intervene physically?”
02 / THE SOLUTION

A clinical sandbox
for movement.

ORTHO-SIM aims to create a digital representation of the patient and their movement, then allow clinicians to explore possible interventions and their biomechanical consequences.

03 / VALIDATION

How do we know
ORTHO-SIM works?

We are not trying to prove the concept with a beautiful animation. We need evidence.

Our validation pathway connects the digital model to clinical judgement and, ultimately, to real-world patient outcomes.

01

Technical validation

Can we reproduce patient anatomy, posture and movement with sufficient accuracy for the intended use?

02

Clinical validation

Do experienced O&P clinicians judge the simulated intervention and biomechanical response as clinically plausible?

03

Real-world validation

When an intervention is physically fabricated and fitted, does the observed outcome correspond with what ORTHO-SIM predicted?

04 / TEST CASE 001
INITIAL CLINICAL USE CASE

One patient.
Two possible
interventions.

An 11-year-old boy presented with lower-limb weakness, suspected quadriceps weakness, knee hyperextension and an abnormal ankle/foot movement pattern.

A KAFO was prescribed for lower-limb control. After fabrication and fitting, the patient was unable to use it successfully.

The clinical question:

Could controlling the ground-reaction force through a suitably designed AFO influence the knee and improve gait without restricting the entire limb?
